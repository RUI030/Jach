# Learning From Failure: Integrating Negative Examples when Fine-tuning Large Language Models as Agents

- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/html/2402.11651
- **Authors / org:** Renxi Wang, Haonan Li, Xudong Han, Yixuan Zhang, Timothy Baldwin (LibrAI; MBZUAI; University of Melbourne)
- **Date:** February 2024 (v2 revised 16 April 2024)
- **Type:** paper
- **Read:** full text

## Summary

Standard practice when fine-tuning agents is to keep only successful trajectories. This paper asks whether failed trajectories are worth keeping, and how. Trajectories are generated by GPT-3.5 as a ReAct agent on maths (with a calculator tool) and question answering (with search), and labelled positive or negative by comparing the final answer with the ground truth. Students are Llama-2-Chat 7B and 13B.

Three training regimes are compared: positives only; positives plus negatives mixed without distinction; and negative-aware training (NAT), where a short tag is added to the prompt saying whether the following trajectory is a correct or an incorrect solution. At inference the "correct" tag is always used.

On maths, with 2k positives, the 7B model averaged 55.9% with positives only, 63.4% with untagged mixing and 64.6% with NAT. With 5k positives the gaps shrink (64.2 / 65.0 / 67.4). For the 13B model with 5k positives, untagged mixing fell below the positives-only baseline (69.1 against 70.8) while NAT stayed above it (71.3). On StrategyQA the 7B model went from 55.4 to 65.8 with NAT. Splitting negatives into two quality bands with separate tags helped further on HotpotQA.

The quality of negatives is decisive. Negatives from GPT-3.5 gave +8.7 points at 2k positives; negatives produced by a fine-tuned Llama-2 7B gave -3.2, and -6.2 at 5k positives. The tag's wording was irrelevant: random strings worked as well as meaningful labels, so the benefit comes from separating the two sets. The analysis suggests negatives mostly improve the reasoning steps while raising action errors somewhat (3.6% with positives only, 10.5% untagged, 7.9% with NAT).

Benefit rose with the number of negatives up to roughly 11k for 2k positives, then levelled off. The method needs ground-truth labels, and it was not tested on coding.

## Key points

- Failed trajectories from a strong model contain useful reasoning; discarding them wastes data when positives are scarce.
- Tag them. Untagged mixing can end up worse than positives only.
- Gains shrink as the number of positives grows.
- Failed trajectories from a weak model hurt, by 3 to 6 points.
- Negatives increase action errors even when tagged.
- "Negative" here means a wrong final answer, not a recovery from an error within a trajectory.

## Relevance to JacKnife

(Our interpretation, not the source's claims.)

- Do not add the 9B model's own failed runs to its training data, tagged or not; this matches the warnings in `escalation/on-policy-expert-corrections.md`.
- Failed trajectories from the admin model are a different matter: tagged as unsuccessful, they may add value while successful Jac trajectories are few.
- An outcome tag in the prompt (task met acceptance criteria or not) is cheap to add to our data format and gives control at inference.
- The rise in action errors is a caution, since tool-call correctness is our weak point. Measure tool-error rate, not only task success, when trying this.
- This paper does not cover recovery examples (error then correction inside one trajectory); for that see the DAgger-style sources under `escalation/`.
